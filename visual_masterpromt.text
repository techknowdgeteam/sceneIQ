3. PRIMARY OBJECTIVE
Read and analyze the COMPLETE ORIGINAL STORY from beginning to end.
Then read and analyze the COMPLETE CHARACTER REFERENCE SHEET.
Your task is to turn the story into a complete visual sequence.
The final result must show:
WHAT HAPPENED → WHO DID IT → HOW THEY DID IT → HOW THEY REACTED → HOW OTHER CHARACTERS REACTED → WHAT OBJECTS WERE INVOLVED → WHAT CHANGED → WHAT THE NEXT VISUAL MOMENT IS.
Do NOT summarize the story into only major scenes.
Do NOT create only one image per scene.
Do NOT skip smaller actions.
Do NOT skip facial reactions.
Do NOT skip important body language.
Do NOT skip object interactions.
Do NOT skip meaningful transitions.

4. THE MOST IMPORTANT RULE — BREAK EVERY MAJOR SCENE INTO INDIVIDUAL VISUAL MOMENTS
This is the central instruction of this master prompt.
Every major scene must be broken into all meaningful visual actions and reactions that actually happen within it.
For example, if the story says:
"Grant slammed his glass onto the table, leaned toward Theo, and accused him of embarrassing everyone. Denise lowered her eyes while Theo stared at Grant in disbelief."
DO NOT produce only:
Scene 1: Grant argues with Theo.
Instead produce a sequence such as:
1. Scene 1 — Grant stands at the table confronting Theo.
2. Scene 1 — Grant grips the glass tightly.
3. Scene 1 — Grant slams the glass onto the table.
4. Scene 1 — The glass hits the table and wine splashes.
5. Scene 1 — Grant leans forward toward Theo with an angry expression.
6. Scene 1 — Theo reacts with stunned disbelief.
7. Scene 1 — Denise lowers her eyes, visibly hurt.
8. Scene 1 — Theo turns toward Denise and notices her reaction.
Every one of those can become a separate image/reference moment if visually useful.
The numbering must remain continuous:
1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8...
Do NOT restart numbering when moving to a new scene.

5. CONTINUOUS NUMBERING IS MANDATORY
The entire output must use ONE continuous numbering system.
Example:
Scene 1
Scene 1 — Establishing moment.
Scene 1 — Character action.
Scene 1 — Object interaction.
Scene 1 — Facial reaction.
Scene 1 — Other character reaction.
Scene 2
Scene 2 — Establishing moment.
Scene 2 — Character action.
Scene 2 — Reaction.
Scene 2 — Dialogue moment.
Scene 2 — Emotional reaction.
Scene 3
Scene 3 — Establishing moment.
Scene 3 — Action.
Scene 3 — Reaction.
Continue numbering until the END of the story.
Do NOT restart at 1 for every scene.

6. DO NOT FORCE AN ARBITRARY NUMBER OF SHOTS
Do not decide in advance:
20 images.
30 images.
50 images.
100 images.
Instead, analyze the story and determine how many visual moments are actually required.
A scene containing many actions may require many image steps.
A simple scene may require fewer.
The final number must be determined by the story itself.
The goal is:
COMPLETE VISUAL COVERAGE, NOT AN ARBITRARY IMAGE COUNT.

Do not compress a sequence of meaningful physical actions into one generic image.
For example:

9. DO NOT INVENT ACTIONS
This rule is equally important.
You must NEVER create actions simply to increase the number of images.
Do NOT add:
Random hugs.
Random touching.
Random walking.
Random crying.
Random smiling.
Random pointing.
Random sitting.
Random standing.
Random phone calls.
Random object interactions.
Every visual action must come from:
The story itself, OR
A natural physical transition required to visually represent an action explicitly described in the story.
Do not alter the story.

10. REACTIONS ARE JUST AS IMPORTANT AS ACTIONS
Whenever a meaningful action occurs, check whether another character reacts.
If the story says:
"Theo slammed the glass down."
Ask:
Does Denise react?
Does Grant react?
Do the other guests react?
Does Theo himself change expression?
Does the object react?
Does the environment change?
If those reactions are described or naturally necessary to represent the story moment, create visual steps for them.

Do not say:
"He interacts with the phone."
Say:
"Theo grips the phone in his right hand, his thumb hovering over the message while his eyes remain fixed on the screen."
Do not say:
"She uses the folder."

"Denise opens the dark folder with both hands and spreads the financial documents across the desk."
The image generator must understand exactly what is happening.

20. STRICT WARDROBE CONTINUITY
This is MANDATORY.
Before generating the visual breakdown, determine every major event.
For each event, create an internal:
WARDROBE LOCK.
Once clothing is established for a continuous event, it MUST remain the same throughout that event.
Maintain:
Clothing items.
Clothing colors.
Clothing style.
Shoes.
Jewelry.
Accessories.
Handbags.
Jackets.
Coats.
Ties.
Shirts.
Dresses.
Pants.
Do NOT randomly change clothing between visual steps.

21. ONE EVENT = ONE WARDROBE
If Scene 1 through Scene 10 all happen during the same event, the characters should continue wearing the same clothing.
Changing the visual step does NOT allow changing the outfit.
Changing the camera does NOT allow changing the outfit.
Changing the character's pose does NOT allow changing the outfit.
Changing the image does NOT allow changing the outfit.
Only a legitimate story event allows a wardrobe change.

22. NATURAL CLOTHING ADJUSTMENTS ARE ALLOWED
There is an important distinction.
A character may naturally:
Remove a coat.
Put on a coat.
Loosen a tie.
Roll up sleeves.
Remove a scarf.
Put down a handbag.
if the story logically causes it.
This is NOT considered a new outfit.
However, the underlying clothing identity and colors must remain consistent.

23. WARDROBE CHANGES REQUIRE A STORY-BASED REASON
A completely different outfit can appear only when there is:
A new day.
A new event.
A significant time jump.
A new season.
Explicit wardrobe change.
Going home to change.
Another legitimate story reason.
If there is no reason:
DO NOT CHANGE THE OUTFIT.

24. CLOTHING MUST MATCH EVENT + SEASON + WEATHER
Analyze:
Event type.
Time.
Season.
Weather.
Environment.
Clothing must logically fit all four.
Do not place winter clothing in a hot summer environment.
Do not dress someone casually at a formal event unless the story supports it.

25. CLOTHING COLORS MUST BE EXPLICITLY REPEATED
Do not assume the image generator remembers previous images.
For every visible character, the final image-generation prompt must explicitly repeat the current locked outfit and colors.
Example:
"Denise Bennett, maintaining her established appearance, wearing the same navy fitted dress, silver necklace, silver earrings, and black heels established for the Friday evening event..."
Do this every time Denise appears during that event.

26. DO NOT USE VAGUE CLOTHING LANGUAGE
BAD:
"Denise wears the same outfit."
GOOD:
"Denise wears the same navy fitted knee-length dress, silver necklace, silver earrings, and black heels."
The actual clothing must be repeated.

Reception hall → hallway → kitchen → parking lot → reception hall.

31. VISUAL VARIETY MUST COME FROM ACTION, NOT RANDOM CLOTHING

32. EACH VISUAL STEP MUST REPRESENT A REAL STORY MOMENT
Every numbered step must answer:
What exactly is happening in this image?
If the answer is vague, rewrite the step.
Bad:
"The characters are emotional."
Good:
"Denise remains seated at the table, her eyes glossy with tears as she looks toward Theo, while her fingers tighten around the edge of the napkin."

33. EACH VISUAL STEP MUST BE IN CHRONOLOGICAL ORDER
The sequence must follow the exact order of events.
Do not show:
A reaction before the action that caused it.
A character leaving before they enter.
An object being broken before it is handled.
A revelation before its setup.
A character knowing information before discovering it.
The visual sequence must follow the story timeline.

34. USE ESTABLISHING SHOTS WHEN NECESSARY
At the beginning of a new major location or event, create an establishing visual when it helps the audience understand where the story has moved.
Examples:

Interior of reception.
Office.
Boardroom.

Parking area.

35. DO NOT OVERUSE ESTABLISHING SHOTS
Do not create an establishing image for every small movement.
Use them when:
A new major location begins.
A new event begins.
The environment is important.

36. IMPORTANT DIALOGUE MUST BE VISUALIZED
When a character says something important, create the visual moment surrounding the dialogue.
Do NOT put the dialogue as text inside the image.
Example:
"Get out of my house."
Visualize:
Character standing.
Arm position.
Facial expression.
Body language.
Other character's reaction.
Environment.
The voice-over supplies the dialogue.

37. CHARACTER DIALOGUE REACTIONS
When dialogue causes an emotional reaction, visualize both sides.
Example:
Character A delivers an accusation.
Visual moments may include:
Character A speaking.
Character B listening.
Character B's expression changing.
Character B reacting physically.
Character A observing the reaction.

38. MICRO-ACTIONS SHOULD BE INCLUDED WHEN THEY MATTER

THUMBNAIL EXPLANATION:

IMPORTANT: The Thumbnail is a separate, video-level YouTube thumbnail prompt. Follow ALL instructions below before generating the scene shotlist:

A. PURPOSE AND PRIORITY
The "Thumbnail" field is a dedicated YouTube thumbnail image-generation prompt for the COMPLETE video/story. It is NOT an ordinary scene image, not a summary image, not a poster containing every event, and not simply the first scene. Its only job is to create the most compelling, truthful, instantly understandable visual packaging for the video so that the intended viewer feels a strong reason to click.

You MUST read and understand the COMPLETE narration/transcript from beginning to end before deciding what the thumbnail should show. Do not choose a thumbnail from the opening lines alone. Identify the story's strongest click-worthy emotional or curiosity moment: the most surprising discovery, confrontation, betrayal, secret, reversal, dilemma, emotional consequence, unusual situation, or unanswered question. Prefer the moment that makes a viewer think "What happened?", "Why would they do that?", or "How will this end?" without giving away the full answer.

The thumbnail must accurately represent the actual story. You may use a visually powerful moment from later in the story, combine story-supported details into one clear composition, or use a symbolic/proverb-like visual when the narration is reflective or conceptual. However, do not invent a person, event, accusation, object, location, relationship, or outcome that the story does not support. Do not create false clickbait. Do not reveal the final resolution or the answer to the central mystery when preserving that information would make the viewer want to watch.

B. CHOOSE THE RIGHT THUMBNAIL TYPE — TEXT OR NO TEXT
Do NOT automatically add text to every thumbnail. Decide deliberately whether the visual is stronger with text or without it. There is no universal rule that text always gets more clicks or that text-free thumbnails always perform better. The correct choice depends on the story, the strength of the image, the audience, and whether a few words add useful context.

Use a NO-TEXT thumbnail when:
- One expressive face, striking action, unusual object, or instantly readable situation already communicates the story's emotional promise.
- Text would repeat what the image already says, clutter the composition, or weaken the mystery.
- A powerful visual contrast or visual question can make the viewer curious without explanation.
- The image needs to feel like a dramatic cinematic still rather than a designed poster.

Use a SHORT-TEXT thumbnail when:
- The image alone may not make the central conflict or unusual relationship clear enough.
- A few words can add a new piece of context, sharpen the emotional stakes, or create a specific unanswered question.
- The text adds information that is NOT already obvious from the image and does NOT simply repeat the video title.
- The story's strongest hook is a short confession, accusation, discovery, contradiction, or emotional statement.

When text is used, aim for approximately 2–4 powerful words; use more only when absolutely necessary for clarity. Keep it brief, large, readable at phone size, and grammatically natural. Examples of hook styles (use only if supported by the actual story): "HE LIED TO ME", "MY OWN DAUGHTER?", "I FOUND OUT", "AFTER 25 YEARS", "HIS BIGGEST SECRET", "I WALKED AWAY". These are examples of style, NOT reusable default text. Create original wording from the current story. Do not use generic phrases such as "YOU WON'T BELIEVE THIS" unless the story genuinely supports that exact promise. Do not put the full title, narration, dialogue paragraph, subtitles, or a long sentence on the thumbnail. Never add text just to fill empty space.

C. SELECT THE SINGLE STRONGEST VISUAL IDEA
Before writing the prompt, silently compare possible thumbnail moments from the entire story and choose ONE primary concept. Choose a clear focal subject and one readable visual story. The thumbnail should communicate one strong emotional beat, not attempt to illustrate the whole plot.

Prioritize, where story-supported:
- A highly readable human face with a genuine, story-appropriate expression.
- A meaningful interaction between two characters, such as confrontation, rejection, disbelief, betrayal, discovery, or a tense exchange.
- A specific story object that creates curiosity, such as a phone, photograph, letter, envelope, key, suitcase, document, ring, or other important item.
- A striking contrast between what a character expects and what they discover.
- A visual situation that raises a question without showing the answer.

A face is useful when emotion is central, but do not force a face into every thumbnail. If an object, location, silhouette, unusual composition, or symbolic image communicates the story more powerfully, use that instead. Avoid crowded group compositions. Usually use one dominant subject and, only when useful, one secondary character or one unmistakable story object. Every included element must have a clear purpose.

D. EMOTION, EXPRESSION, AND BODY LANGUAGE
Choose the exact emotion that best sells the story's central tension: shock, disbelief, suspicion, betrayal, fear, heartbreak, humiliation, anger, determination, relief, or another emotion genuinely supported by the story. Make the expression readable at small size. Describe the eyes, eyebrows, mouth, jaw, head angle, shoulders, hands, and posture precisely where visible.

Do not make every character scream, widen their eyes unnaturally, or look directly at the camera. Avoid exaggerated, artificial, generic "shocked face" expressions. Use believable, emotionally specific expressions. A restrained, devastated expression may be more compelling than an exaggerated scream. If two characters appear, make their emotional contrast clear: for example, one composed while the other is visibly shaken, or one confronting while the other avoids eye contact.

E. COMPOSITION AND VISUAL HIERARCHY
Design the image specifically for a YouTube thumbnail, not as a random 16:9 frame from the video. Use a landscape 16:9 composition. Establish a clear visual hierarchy:
1. The primary face, action, or story object must be the first thing the eye notices.
2. A secondary character or clue may support the story, but must not compete with the focal point.
3. The background should establish useful context while remaining simple and uncluttered.
4. If text is selected, reserve clean negative space for it and position it so it does not cover faces, eyes, hands, or the key object.

Use strong subject separation, deliberate framing, readable silhouettes, clear foreground/background separation, and enough contrast for the image to remain understandable when viewed very small on a mobile screen. Use close-up or medium close-up framing when facial emotion is the hook; use a wider composition only when the physical situation or environment is essential to understanding the story. Do not place every subject in the center by default. Use a deliberate composition that balances the focal subject, any secondary clue, and optional text.

F. COLOR, LIGHTING, AND CINEMATIC IMPACT
Use a deliberate color and lighting treatment that reinforces the story's emotional beat. Use controlled contrast, rich but believable color, dimensional lighting, and clear separation between subject and background. Warm or golden light may support nostalgia, intimacy, success, or revelation; cool gray-blue tones may support isolation, tension, uncertainty, or sadness; sharper contrast may support confrontation or danger. These are creative options, not fixed rules. Match the palette to the story rather than applying the same color grade to every thumbnail.

Use motivated light sources and believable light direction. Where appropriate, include a strong edge/rim light, window light, practical lamps, street lighting, or a controlled cinematic key light. Light should help reveal the face and key object, not wash out details. Avoid muddy shadows, flat exposure, excessive glow, random colored rays, or lighting that contradicts the story's setting and time.

G. CHARACTER AND STORY CONTINUITY
Use the established character descriptions and visual references as the authority for identity. Any character shown must retain the correct face, age, skin tone, ethnicity, hairstyle, build, and other defining features. Wardrobe must be appropriate to the actual story event and consistent with the relevant scene's locked clothing. Do not change a character's identity or clothing merely to make the thumbnail more attractive.

The thumbnail may use a dramatic composition that is not an exact frame from the scene list, but it must remain faithful to the story's facts, characters, relationships, objects, and timeline. It may intensify visual clarity and emotional readability, but must not falsify what happened. If the story uses a cartoon/webtoon visual style, the thumbnail MUST use that same style and the chosen simple dot-eye design; do not switch to photorealistic live-action. If the project specifies another visual style, preserve that style consistently.

H. TEXT DESIGN RULES WHEN TEXT IS SELECTED
The generated thumbnail prompt must specify the exact wording, spelling, capitalization, and placement of the chosen hook. Text must be large, bold, high-contrast, clean, and legible on a phone. Use a simple strong type treatment with a subtle outline or shadow only when it improves readability. Keep text away from the main face and important objects. Use no more than one short hook. Do not add logos, channel names, fake platform badges, extra labels, decorative paragraphs, watermarks, or unrelated icons unless the user explicitly requests them.

If the image-generation system is unreliable at rendering exact typography, still specify the intended text clearly in the prompt, but do not compensate by adding more words. The output must never contain misspelled, duplicated, garbled, or random lettering.
